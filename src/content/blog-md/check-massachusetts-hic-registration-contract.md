Most bad contractor stories start the same way: a handshake deal, a big deposit and a phone number that stops answering. Massachusetts has a law written for this problem. The Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) law, M.G.L. c. 142A, requires most contractors who work on homes to register with the state, and it sets out what a written contract has to include.

This guide shows how to check a registration in a few minutes and what to look for in the contract before you sign.

## Who has to be registered

The state Office of Consumer Affairs and Business Regulation (OCABR) says the law requires contractors working on **existing 1 to 4 unit, owner-occupied primary residences** to be registered. That covers most siding, window, door and trim jobs on a home you live in.

The state is also clear about what happens if you hire someone who is not registered: you will not be protected by the Home Improvement Contractor Law.

### HIC registration and a construction supervisor license are different

A Montague building department FAQ explains the difference. A Construction Supervisor License (CSL) is required for work that involves structural elements, and the holder has passed an exam on the building code. An HIC registrant does not take an exam, but registers with the state and pays into the Guaranty Fund. The same FAQ says that since July 1, 2008, roof replacement, siding and window replacement require both an HIC registration and a CSL. The two can be held by different people working on the same job.

## How to look up a registration

1. **Get the registration number.** Ask for it, and look for it on the estimate or contract. Chapter 142A requires the contractor's registration number to appear in the contract.
2. **Search the state lookup.** OCABR's public lookup is at [services.oca.state.ma.us/hic](https://services.oca.state.ma.us/hic), and the state now also points homeowners to the MA Contractor Hub at [ContractorHub.mass.gov](https://contractorhub.mass.gov) to research registration status and disciplinary history.
3. **Match the details.** The name and address on the registration should match the business you are hiring.
4. **Check arbitration and Guaranty Fund history.** OCABR offers a separate search for a contractor's arbitration and Guaranty Fund history.
5. **Confirm the status.** Look at whether the registration shows as active and when it expires, and ask about anything that looks off.

For example, Maia Construction's registration is HIC #204634. You can enter that number in the state lookup and see the record yourself, which is exactly what you should do with any contractor you are considering.

> If a contractor is not registered, the state says you will not be protected by the Home Improvement Contractor Law. To report an unregistered contractor, the state lists HICRegistration@mass.gov.

## What the contract must include

Section 2 of Chapter 142A says every residential contracting agreement over $1,000 must be in writing. It lists what the contract must contain. In plain terms:

- **The complete agreement**, including any documents it refers to.
- **Names, addresses and the contractor's registration number.**
- **A start date and a substantial completion date.**
- **A detailed description of the work and the materials** to be used.
- **The total price and a payment schedule** with the dollar amount of each payment.
- **Permit disclosure**, stating which permits are needed and that the contractor is responsible for getting them.
- **Your rights**, including where to direct questions about registration, your three-day right to cancel where it applies, any warranties and whether the contract creates a lien or security interest on your home.
- **A warning** above the signature line not to sign if there are any blank spaces.
- **Signatures of all parties**, with a signed copy given to you. Work is not supposed to begin before that happens.

### Deposits

The law caps the advance deposit at the greater of **one-third of the total contract price** or the **cost of special-order materials** that must be bought before work begins. Final payment cannot be demanded until the work is completed to the satisfaction of the parties.

If a contractor asks for half up front on a job with no special-order materials, ask how that fits the law.

### Arbitration clauses

A contract can include an optional dispute resolution or arbitration clause, but Chapter 142A requires it to be clearly disclosed and separately signed and dated by each party. Read it before you initial it.

### A missing term does not cancel the contract

The statute says a contract that fails these requirements is not invalid for that reason alone. The requirements still matter: they protect you if there is a disagreement later.

## The Guaranty Fund and permits

Registered contractors pay into a Guaranty Fund run by OCABR, and homeowners may be eligible for reimbursement from it in certain cases under 201 CMR 14. We have seen different maximum amounts quoted online, so check the current limit directly with OCABR before relying on any number.

One detail can cost you that protection. Chapter 142A says homeowners who pull their own permits are excluded from the Guaranty Fund. If a contractor asks you to get the permit for their job, keep that in mind.

## A quick checklist before you sign

- Registration number looked up and active
- Name and address match the business
- Written contract for any job over $1,000
- Start date, completion date and full scope of work and materials
- Payment schedule with dollar amounts, deposit within the legal limit
- Permit responsibility stated as the contractor's
- No blank spaces, and a signed copy in your hands
