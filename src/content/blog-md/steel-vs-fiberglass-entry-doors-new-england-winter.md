In January you can feel it: a cold draft at the threshold, frost on the inside of the door's glass, maybe a door that sticks in damp weather and rattles in dry cold. If your entry door is near the end of its life, the usual choices for a replacement are steel and fiberglass. Both are common, both can be insulated, and the differences that matter in a Massachusetts winter are less about the skin and more about the glass, the frame, the weatherstripping and the installation.

This article walks through what ENERGY STAR measures for doors, how steel and fiberglass differ in care, and what to ask before you choose.

## What ENERGY STAR rates on a door

ENERGY STAR does not certify doors by material. Under its Version 7.0 specification for residential windows, doors and skylights, effective October 23, 2023, swinging doors are rated by how much glass they have:

- **Opaque (no glass):** U-factor of 0.17 or lower.
- **Half-lite glass or less:** U-factor of 0.23 or lower.
- **More than half-lite glass:** U-factor of 0.26 or lower in the Northern and North-Central zones.

ENERGY STAR's climate zone spreadsheet places every Massachusetts ZIP code in the Northern zone. The specification also requires swinging doors to have an air leakage rating of 0.5 cfm per square foot or less, shown on the NFRC label or through an accepted certification label.

U-factor measures heat loss through the whole door unit, and lower is better. So the first thing that separates two doors is often the amount and type of glass, and only then the slab material.

> Compare doors by their NFRC rating and glass level first. A solid steel door and a solid fiberglass door can both qualify.

## Steel doors: what to know for winter

A steel entry door has a steel skin. Therma-Tru's finishing guidance for its fiberglass and steel door systems points to the main maintenance issue: rust.

- Therma-Tru says its steel doors, unless purchased with a factory-applied finish, ship with only a factory-applied primer that works as a temporary rust retardant.
- It recommends that steel door systems be finished within several days of installation or exposure to the weather.
- If deep scratches score through the primer, or rust appears, the guide calls for mild sanding to remove the rust and then priming before painting.
- It recommends inspecting steel doors for finish deterioration at least once a year, and more often in severe exposures such as within about 5 miles of salt water.

The practical takeaway: keep the paint intact, especially along the bottom edge where snow, salt and boots wear it, and deal with scratches before rust starts.

## Fiberglass doors: what to know for winter

A fiberglass entry door has a fiberglass skin, smooth on some lines and textured like wood grain on others. The same Therma-Tru guide gives fiberglass a longer finishing window, saying fiberglass door systems should be finished within 6 months. Therma-Tru's guide says some of its fiberglass lines can be stained with its own stain system, while its smooth line should not be stained. The guide is specific about which products and which finishes are allowed, so follow the instructions for the exact door you buy.

Fiberglass does not rust, which removes the main steel concern. It still needs its finish maintained, and its frame, sill and weatherstripping matter as much as on a steel door.

## Dark colors and storm doors

If you want a dark door, or plan to keep a storm door in front of it, check the manufacturer's guidance first. Therma-Tru's guide notes that certain fiberglass doors can be painted a dark color and placed behind a storm door, but warns that with a dark color, the door's surface can get extremely hot in direct sunlight. Other manufacturers publish their own guidance, so check the instructions and warranty for the door you choose before you pick the color.

## Finishing in cold weather

The same guide recommends finishing only when temperatures are between 50°F and 90°F, with humidity below 85%, and not in direct sunlight. If a door goes in during December, ask how and when it will be painted or stained. A factory-finished door avoids that question.

## Where winter drafts really come from

Drafts can come from several places besides the door slab itself:

- **Weatherstripping** that is torn, flattened or missing.
- **The threshold and door sweep**, which take the most wear.
- **The frame**, if it is out of square and the door no longer closes evenly against the seals.
- **The gap between the frame and the rough opening.** Therma-Tru's installation hints call for caulking around the entire unit on the weather side and adding insulation to the cavity between the opening and the unit to reduce air infiltration and heat transfer.
- **Rot in the existing frame or sill**, which no new slab can fix.

If the frame, sill or seals are the problem, a new slab alone will not fix it. A pre-hung unit with a new frame, sill and weatherstripping, installed square and sealed to the opening, addresses those parts too.

## Questions to ask before you buy

1. What are the door's NFRC U-factor and air leakage ratings, and is it ENERGY STAR certified for the Northern zone?
2. How much glass does it have, and what kind?
3. Is it factory-finished, or will it be painted or stained on site? If on site, when?
4. Are dark colors or a storm door allowed under the manufacturer's instructions?
5. Is the quote for a slab only, or a pre-hung unit with a new frame and sill?
6. How will the gap around the frame be insulated and sealed?
7. If the house was built before 1978, how will painted trim and casings be handled? Massachusetts regulates paid work that disturbs painted surfaces in pre-1978 housing under 454 CMR 22.00.
