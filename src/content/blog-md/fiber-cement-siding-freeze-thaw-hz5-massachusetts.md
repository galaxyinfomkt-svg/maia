Fiber cement siding has a reputation for standing up to weather, but in Central Massachusetts the hard part of winter is the back-and-forth. A wall can be wet with snowmelt in the afternoon and frozen that night. If you are pricing fiber cement, you have probably seen the label "HZ5" on quotes or product pages and wondered whether it matters here, and what else decides how the siding holds up.

This article explains what the HardieZone system is, what our local weather actually looks like, and which installation details in the manufacturer's instructions deal with water and cold.

## What the HardieZone system is

James Hardie, one of the fiber cement manufacturers, sells its siding in two climate-based product lines. Its HardieZone page says the company "tailors siding formulations to specific regional climates" and describes the HZ5 line as "right at home in climates with freezing temperatures, extreme seasonal temperature variations, and snow and ice."

The company does not ask you to guess your zone. Its HardieZone page has a ZIP code lookup, and its installation instructions tell installers to confirm they are using the correct HardieZone product instructions before starting, either at hardiezone.com or by calling 1-866-942-7343.

> Before you sign, ask which HardieZone product line is on the quote and check your ZIP code on the manufacturer's lookup.

Other brands of fiber cement have their own product lines and their own instructions. Whatever brand you choose, the same rule applies: the installer should follow that manufacturer's written instructions for your climate.

## What freeze-thaw looks like in Central Massachusetts

To put numbers on it, we pulled daily temperature records for Worcester Regional Airport from the NOAA Regional Climate Centers' ACIS service for 1991 through 2020 and counted two things:

- **Nights at or below 32°F:** about 130 per year on average, ranging from 106 to 153.
- **Days that crossed the freezing line** (a low at or below 32°F and a high above 32°F): about 84 per year on average, ranging from 61 to 110.

That second number is the one to think about. On each of those days, any water sitting in a joint, a cut edge or a gap behind trim had a chance to freeze and thaw again. Your own house may see more or fewer cycles depending on elevation, shade and exposure, but the pattern holds across the region.

## Where water gets in, and what the instructions say about it

Freeze-thaw trouble starts with water that stays where it should not. The HardiePlank lap siding installation requirements (December 2019 edition) include several details aimed at exactly that.

### A water-resistive barrier behind the siding

The instructions say a water-resistive barrier is required in accordance with local building code, and that it must be installed with proper flashing at penetrations and junctions. James Hardie also states it "will assume no responsibility for water infiltration." In practice, the siding sheds most water and the barrier behind it handles what gets past.

### Clearances at the roof, deck, paths and ground

The instructions say all of the clearance details in the drawings must be followed. Those drawings cover where siding meets a roof, a deck, steps and paths, and the ground. The point of each gap is to keep the bottom edge of the siding out of standing water and snow. The same document says finished grade should slope away from the building, typically a minimum of 6 inches in the first 10 feet, and that the products should not be installed where they may remain in contact with standing water.

### Kickout flashing where a roof meets a wall

Where a roof edge ends against a sidewall, the instructions call for kickout flashing, sized as required by the IRC with a minimum of 4 inches by 4 inches in the drawing. Without it, roof runoff can pour behind the siding at that corner, which is one of the more common places to find soaked sheathing.

### Joint treatment at butt joints

The instructions list joint treatment options for butt joints, including joint flashing (which James Hardie recommends), caulking, or an H-jointer cover. Caulk must meet the listed ASTM standards and go on per the caulk maker's written instructions.

### Cut edges and paint

The instructions say to caulk, paint or prime all field-cut edges. For factory-primed products, they say the siding must be painted within 180 days of installation, recommend 100% acrylic topcoats, and say not to paint when wet. Paint application temperature is left to the paint manufacturer's instructions, so ask your contractor which paint will be used and what its label allows.

### Storage before installation

Even before the siding goes on the wall, the instructions say to store it flat, dry and covered, and warn that installing siding wet or saturated may result in shrinkage at butt joints. On a winter or early spring job, that means the material should not sit uncovered in the driveway.

## Questions to ask about a fiber cement quote

Use these when comparing estimates:

1. Which product line is quoted, and has the installer checked your ZIP code against the manufacturer's zone lookup?
2. What water-resistive barrier and flashing are included, and are kickouts shown where roofs meet walls?
3. How are butt joints treated: flashing, caulk or H-jointers?
4. Is the siding factory-finished or primed, and if primed, when will it be painted and with what?
5. Will cut edges be sealed as the instructions require?
6. Where will the material be stored during the job?

A written answer to each of these tells you more about how the siding will handle winter than the brand name alone.

## A note on older houses

If your house was built before 1978, removing the existing siding and trim can disturb paint that may contain lead. Massachusetts regulates that work under 454 CMR 22.00. Ask any contractor how they will handle it before tear-off begins.
