If your house was built before 1978, the paint on your clapboards, trim and window sashes may contain lead. Scraping, cutting or pulling those surfaces off can turn old paint into dust that settles on the ground, the porch and inside the house. Massachusetts has specific rules for paid contractors who do this kind of work, and knowing the basics helps you ask the right questions before anyone starts tearing off siding or replacing windows.

This article explains which jobs are covered, what the contractor must do before work begins, and how to check a license.

## Who sets the rules in Massachusetts

The federal Renovation, Repair and Painting (RRP) Rule from the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency covers paid work that disturbs painted surfaces in homes, child care facilities and preschools built before 1978. EPA lets some states run their own programs, and its RRP page lists Massachusetts as one of them.

In Massachusetts, the program is run by the Department of Labor Standards (DLS) under its regulation, 454 CMR 22.00. The state's guidance to homeowners is direct: if a home was built before 1978 and you are renovating, repairing or painting, you must use a licensed lead-safe renovation contractor.

### Which homes count

The regulation calls covered homes "target housing." It defines that as housing built before 1978, with two exceptions: housing for the elderly or people with disabilities (unless a child under six lives there or is expected to), and zero-bedroom dwellings. Most single-family homes, two-families and three-deckers built before 1978 fall inside the definition.

## Which exterior jobs trigger the rules

Under 454 CMR 22.11, work has to be done as regulated "Renovation Work" when paint has not been tested and the job:

- disturbs more than 20 square feet of painted surface on the exterior of the building, or
- disturbs more than 6 square feet of painted surface per room inside, or
- involves window replacement or partial demolition of painted surfaces.

Two points matter for exterior projects:

1. **Window replacement is covered regardless of size.** The regulation names window replacement on its own, so even a single window swap in a pre-1978 home is regulated work.
2. **Siding and trim jobs pass 20 square feet fast.** Stripping one wall of painted clapboards or replacing a run of rotted fascia and corner boards will usually disturb more than that.

The same section says all Renovation Work must be done by licensed Lead-safe Renovation Contractors, with a narrow exception for entities that hold a DLS contractor licensing waiver to work on their own property with their own employees.

> Window replacement in a pre-1978 Massachusetts home is regulated lead-safe work even if only one window is replaced.

## What the contractor must do before work starts

The state's rules include a notice step before the job. A DLS bulletin for contractors says that no more than 60 days before starting, the contractor must give the owner and any tenants a copy of EPA's **Renovate Right** pamphlet and get written acknowledgment that they received it. State guidance for consumers says the same thing in plain terms: you should get the pamphlet before the work begins.

If you live in a two-family or three-decker, that means your tenants should get the pamphlet too. If nobody hands you one, ask about it.

## Three licenses that are easy to confuse

Massachusetts has several contractor credentials, and they cover different things:

- **Lead-Safe Renovation Contractor license**, issued by DLS for work that disturbs paint in pre-1978 housing. DLS says this license is different from a general contractor's license and from a Construction Supervisor License.
- **Construction Supervisor License (CSL)**, issued by the Department of Public Safety, tied to the building code.
- **Home Improvement Contractor (HIC) registration**, issued by the Office of Consumer Affairs and Business Regulation, which ties into the contract rules and the Guaranty Fund under Chapter 142A.

One credential does not stand in for another. A registered HIC still needs the lead-safe license (or a licensed firm on the job) for regulated work on a pre-1978 home.

DLS also states that Lead-safe Renovation Contractor licenses are valid for five years from the date they are issued, so ask when the license was issued.

## How to check a lead-safe license

DLS says you can call **617-626-6960** to check a Lead-Safe Renovation Contractor's licensing status or violation history. Before you sign:

1. Ask for the company's Lead-Safe Renovation Contractor license number in writing.
2. Call DLS to confirm it is active.
3. Ask who on the crew is trained and will be on site.
4. Confirm that the Renovate Right pamphlet will be provided, and keep your signed acknowledgment.

## What about doing it yourself?

EPA's RRP page says homeowners working on their own pre-1978 home are generally not covered by the federal rule, while landlords, home child care operators and house flippers are. That exemption does not make the dust any safer. If you plan to scrape or cut painted siding yourself, read the EPA and DLS guidance first, and keep children and pets away from the work area.

## Deleading is a separate track

The DLS regulation separates lead-safe renovation from deleading. Its definition of a renovation project excludes deleading work done to comply with the Massachusetts Lead Law (M.G.L. c. 111, § 197), which is its own process. A siding or window project does not make a home deleaded. If a child under six lives in a rental unit you own, or you received a lead order or notice, read the state's Lead Law guidance on mass.gov and resolve that first, because it may change how the exterior work should be planned.

## How to tell if your house is pre-1978

If you are not sure when your home was built, your town assessor's record usually lists a year built. If there is any doubt, plan the job as if the paint contains lead until testing says otherwise.
