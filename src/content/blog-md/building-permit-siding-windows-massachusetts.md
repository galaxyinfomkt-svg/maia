A siding or window job without the right permit can come back to bite you. An inspector can stop the work, and an open or missing permit can come up later when you sell or refinance. Homeowners also lose some consumer protections in Massachusetts depending on who pulls the permit.

The short answer for most towns: plan on a building permit for replacement siding and replacement windows. The longer answer depends on your town, the size of the job and who files the paperwork. Here is how it works.

## What the state building code says

Massachusetts uses one statewide building code, 780 CMR, and every city and town enforces it through a local building department. Section 105.2 of the code lists work that does not need a permit, and it says no application or notice to the building official is required for **ordinary repairs** to structures.

The question is what counts as an ordinary repair. A loose clapboard or a cracked piece of trim is one thing. Stripping a wall to sheathing or swapping out windows is another, and many local building departments treat those as permit work.

## How towns handle siding and windows

Local building department FAQs show that towns read the code a little differently:

- **Lee** says a permit is required for installing windows, whether new or replacement.
- **Tewksbury** says yes to window replacement and notes that it is an energy conservation matter covered by the state building code.
- **Scituate** lists roofing, siding and window replacement among projects that need a permit.
- **Acton** lists roofing and siding work and says the only exception is an ordinary repair.
- **Deerfield** sets a local threshold: a permit for re-siding more than 25% of the house, and a permit for window or exterior door replacement.

The pattern is clear even with the variation. Small patch repairs may not need a permit, while full siding replacement and window replacement usually do. Your town's building department has the final word, so call or check its website before work starts.

> Before work starts, ask the contractor which permits your town requires and get that answer in the written contract.

## Who should pull the permit

This is where Massachusetts law matters most for homeowners.

### The contractor should file

The Home Improvement Contractor law, M.G.L. c. 142A, says the contract must disclose which permits are needed and state that the contractor is responsible for getting them. A Montague building department FAQ puts it plainly: it is the responsibility of the registered HIC to obtain all permits for work covered by the law.

### What happens if you file yourself

If you pull the permit yourself, you fall under what towns call the homeowner exemption. Chapter 142A says homeowners who get their own permits are excluded from the Guaranty Fund, and the Montague FAQ adds that you take on responsibility for the work meeting code. If a contractor asks you to pull the permit for a job they are doing, that is a reason to ask why.

### Two credentials for this kind of work

The same Montague FAQ says that beginning July 1, 2008, people who perform roof replacement, siding and window replacement must hold both a Home Improvement Contractor registration and a Construction Supervisor License. It also says the building official will ask to see both at the time the permit application is filed. The license and registration can be held by different people on the same job, as long as both are in place and listed.

## What the permit process looks like

Details vary by town, but a typical exterior permit goes like this:

1. **Application.** The contractor files with the local building department. If someone other than the owner applies, written authorization from the owner goes with the application.
2. **Review.** The building department checks the application against the code.
3. **Permit card.** Once approved, a permit card is issued and must be posted at the job site until the work is done.
4. **Inspections.** The contractor schedules inspections as the job progresses. The Montague FAQ lists exterior siding among the stages that are typically inspected.
5. **Final sign-off.** The inspector closes out the permit at the end.

Ask for a copy of the permit and the final inspection record. Keep them with your house papers.

## Situations that add steps

A few common cases can add requirements beyond the building permit:

- **Homes built before 1978.** Window replacement and larger siding or trim jobs fall under Massachusetts lead-safe renovation rules. See our guide to [lead paint rules for siding and window work](/blog/lead-safe-siding-window-work-pre-1978-massachusetts).
- **Suspected asbestos siding.** Removal or disturbance of asbestos cement siding can require advance notice to MassDEP. See [asbestos cement siding options](/blog/asbestos-cement-siding-massachusetts-options).
- **Local historic districts.** If your house sits in a local historic district, ask the town whether exterior changes need review by the historic district commission before the building permit.
- **Changing window sizes.** Making an opening larger or smaller means cutting or adding wall framing. Describe that clearly on the application so the building department reviews the right scope.

## Questions to ask before you sign

- Which permits does my town require for this job?
- Who will file them, and under whose license and registration?
- Will the permit number and inspection dates be shared with me?
- Is the cost of permits included in the contract price?
