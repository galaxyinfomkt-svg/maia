Cedar shingles are part of the look of New England. They are also the siding homeowners ask about most when something starts to change: one wall turning dark, shingles cupping or splitting, black streaks around nails, green growth on the shady side. The first question is usually "How long is this supposed to last?"

There is no single honest number. A U.S. Forest Service guide on shake and shingle siding, written with the Cedar Shake and Shingle Bureau, says that wood shakes and shingles, if properly installed, finished and maintained, will age gracefully and provide a long service life. Each of those three conditions is something you can check on your own house.

## What decides how long cedar lasts

### 1. The grade of the shingle

The Forest Service guide is blunt: the first requirement for long service life is the selection of the shingle, and the higher the grade, the longer the siding can be expected to last.

- **No. 1 grade** western redcedar shingles must be 100% clear, 100% heartwood and 100% vertical grain.
- Shingles that contain only heartwood are more resistant to decay than shingles with sapwood.
- Vertical-grain shingles are less likely to warp and split than flat-grain ones.
- Narrow shingles are less likely than wide ones, and thick shingles less likely than thin ones, to warp and split.

Eastern white cedar, also called northern white cedar, is also used for sidewall shingles. The guide notes it has its own grade rules and is approved for sidewalls, but it is not covered by the Cedar Shake and Shingle Bureau's certification standards.

### 2. How it was installed

The guide describes two main methods:

- **Direct application** over a properly installed and flashed weather barrier.
- **Rain screen**, where furring and horizontal nailing strips hold the shingles off the sheathing, leaving a vented space behind them so the wall can drain and dry.

It also shows shingles that buckled because they were installed with too little gap between them. Cedar moves with moisture, and it needs room to do it.

### 3. The nails

Fasteners matter more than most people expect. The guide calls for two corrosion-resistant nails per shingle (four for shingles wider than 10 inches), placed 3/4 inch from each edge and 1 inch above the exposure line. It recommends hot-dipped galvanized nails that meet ASTM A153 or type 304 or 316 stainless steel.

Those dark blue-black streaks around nails are a clue. The guide explains that iron reacts with extractives in cedar to make that stain, and it often appears when the zinc coating on galvanized nails weathers away.

### 4. The finish, and keeping it up

Finish choice sets your maintenance schedule. According to the guide:

- Tinted water-repellent preservatives give roughly 2 to 3 years of service on a single-story house with a wide roof overhang, depending on exposure.
- Semitransparent stains usually last 4 to 5 years on the south side and more than 10 years on the north side.
- Solid-color stains and paint form a film. Cedar left in sunlight for as little as 2 to 3 weeks will not hold film-forming finishes as well as an unweathered surface.

That north-south difference is why one side of a cedar house often looks tired years before the others. The guide says it is often necessary to put different sides of the house on different refinishing cycles.

> Uneven weathering from one wall to the next is normal for cedar. Plan refinishing by side, starting with the walls that get the most sun.

## Reading the warning signs

### Mold and algae

Green or black growth is common on shady walls. The guide says mold and algae do not degrade the wood and removing them is a cosmetic issue. It recommends gentle cleaners with oxygen bleach (sodium percarbonate), applied and left wet for 12 to 15 minutes, then rinsed with a garden hose pointed down so water is not forced behind the courses.

### Pressure washing

The guide warns that in the wrong hands, high-pressure water can strip away enough wood fiber to drastically shorten the life of the shingles. If pressure washing is truly needed, it should be done only by someone experienced with cedar.

### Old paint

If the shingles were painted or stained before about 1985, the guide warns the finish may contain lead and says not to sand it. On homes built before 1978, paid work that disturbs paint also falls under Massachusetts [lead-safe renovation rules](/blog/lead-safe-siding-window-work-pre-1978-massachusetts).

## Repair, refinish or replace?

Use what you see to decide:

- **Refinish** if the shingles are sound and flat, and the problem is fading, graying or uneven color.
- **Repair** if damage is local: a few split or cupped shingles, rot near a downspout, a corner that takes splash from the roof. Individual shingles and small sections can be replaced, though new wood will look different from weathered wood until it ages or is finished to match.
- **Replace** if splitting, cupping and soft wood are widespread, if nails are failing across whole walls, or if water is getting behind the siding. Replacement also lets you check the sheathing, flashing and weather barrier, and choose an installation method such as rain screen.
