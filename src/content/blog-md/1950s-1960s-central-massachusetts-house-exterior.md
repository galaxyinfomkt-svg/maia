You bought a ranch, a Cape or a split-level built in the 1950s or 1960s, or you have lived in one for years. The outside looks tired, a few windows fog up, the trim is peeling, and you are not sure what is original, what was covered over, and what to tackle first. Houses from that era come with a few rules, and possibly a few materials, worth knowing about before anyone starts pulling siding off.

This article is a checklist for the exterior of a mid-century house in Central Massachusetts, in the order we would think about it.

## How many houses we are talking about

The U.S. Census Bureau's American Community Survey (2020 to 2024 five-year estimates) counts 355,185 housing units in Worcester County. Of those, 38,338 are in buildings built in the 1950s and 31,792 in the 1960s, about one in five units in the county. Including everything built before 1980, the share is close to two in three.

If you live in one of these houses, you have a lot of company.

## 1. Find out what is under the surface

Before you choose a new siding, know what is on the wall now. A mid-century house may have the original wood siding or shingles, a later layer of aluminum or vinyl over them, or cement-based shingles. Each one changes the job.

### Lead paint

Any house built before 1978 may have lead paint on siding, trim, windows and doors. In Massachusetts, paid work that disturbs paint in pre-1978 housing is regulated under 454 CMR 22.00, run by the Department of Labor Standards. DLS says a licensed lead-safe renovation contractor must be used for renovation, repair or painting in a home built before 1978. The regulation treats window replacement as covered work regardless of size, and exterior work that disturbs more than 20 square feet of painted surface is covered too.

If a child under six lives in the home, the Massachusetts Lead Law adds owner obligations; see the state's Lead Law page.

### Possible asbestos cement siding or roofing

The EPA lists roofing and siding shingles and asbestos cement products among products that may contain asbestos. You generally cannot tell by looking. The EPA's advice is that materials in good condition that are not disturbed are not likely to pose a health risk, and that if in doubt, you should treat the material as if it contains asbestos and leave it alone.

If removal is part of the plan, Massachusetts has its own process. MassDEP's AQ 04 asbestos notification must be filed ten working days before asbestos removal work starts, through the eDEP online system, with a fee listed at $100 per notification on the state's page. DLS requires the notification to be filed by the licensed asbestos abatement contractor. Testing first tells you which path you are on.

## 2. Check the wall behind the siding

Old siding can hide soft sheathing, damaged framing at sills, and gaps around windows. The vinyl siding installation manual published by the Polymeric Exterior Products Association says not to install vinyl siding over rotting wood and to replace rotten boards first. Fiber cement instructions from James Hardie require a water-resistive barrier installed with flashing as required by local code. Either way, the wall has to be sound and wrapped before the new siding goes on.

Ask any contractor what happens if they find rot after tear-off: how it will be priced and approved.

## 3. Windows

Original windows from the 1950s and 1960s, or replacement windows from decades ago, may show their age with fogged glass, drafts and painted-shut sashes. When you compare replacement quotes:

- Look for the **NFRC label** ratings: U-factor, SHGC, visible transmittance and air leakage.
- ENERGY STAR places all of Massachusetts in its **Northern** zone. Under Version 7.0, windows qualify there with a U-factor of 0.22 or lower and an SHGC of 0.17 or higher, or through an equivalent path set out in the specification.
- In a pre-1978 house, window replacement is regulated lead-safe work under 454 CMR 22.00.

Doing windows and siding in the same project lets the window flashing be tied into the new water-resistive barrier while the wall is open.

## 4. Trim, fascia and soffits

If you see peeling paint or soft spots on fascia, rake boards, corner boards or sills, remember that rot needs water, so find the source first: gutters, missing flashing, trim too close to the ground or a roof. Then decide spot by spot whether to replace with wood or cellular PVC. AZEK's installation guide says its PVC trim absorbs almost no moisture, but also that it expands and contracts with temperature, and it sets fastening and paint color rules to manage that.

## 5. Doors

Original or older entry doors may be drafty at the frame, threshold and weatherstripping. ENERGY STAR rates doors by how much glass they have, regardless of material. A pre-hung unit with a new frame and sill addresses more of the draft points than a new slab alone.

## 6. Local rules to check

- **Building permits.** Ask your town's building department what permits siding, window and door work need.
- **Historic districts.** If your house is inside a local historic district created under Chapter 40C, exterior changes may need a certificate from the commission first. The MHC inventory (MACRIS) and your town can tell you.
- **Utility programs.** Check current program rules before choosing windows or insulation upgrades.

## A sensible order of work

For many mid-century houses, a logical sequence is:

1. Test for lead and, if there are suspect cement shingles or roofing, asbestos.
2. Fix water problems: roof leaks, gutters, grading.
3. Replace windows and doors that need it.
4. Repair sheathing and framing, install the water-resistive barrier and flashing.
5. Install siding and trim.
6. Paint or finish, following each product's timing.

Your house may need a different order or only some of these steps. The goal is to avoid paying twice, such as cutting new siding open for windows a year later.
