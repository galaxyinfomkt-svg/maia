Every winter, some Central Massachusetts homeowners find water stains on a ceiling, paint blistering under the eaves or a soft spot in the fascia once the snow melts. The common thread is often an ice dam: a ridge of ice at the roof edge that holds back meltwater until it finds a way into the house.

This article explains how ice dams form, what they do to the trim and siding below them, and the difference between treating the damage and fixing the cause.

## How an ice dam forms

The National Weather Service describes the cycle plainly. Heavy snow on the roof melts during the day and refreezes when temperatures drop at night. The refrozen water builds a ridge at the cold roof edge. Repeated melt and refreeze cycles push water up under the shingles, and from there it can reach the attic and damage ceilings, walls and contents.

What starts the melting is usually heat from the house. University of Minnesota Extension says heat carried by air leaking into the attic is often the main cause, which is why it recommends sealing those air leaks first and then adding insulation. A U.S. Department of Energy resource says a properly sealed and insulated attic can help prevent ice dams.

### Why Central Massachusetts sees them

Snow totals here swing widely from year to year. NOAA climate data for Worcester Regional Airport show calendar-year snowfall between 1991 and 2020 ranging from 33.4 inches in 1998 to 133.1 inches in 1996, with several years above 100 inches. Long cold spells with deep snow on the roof give ice dams time to build.

The state building code also treats ice damming as a real risk. Table R301.2(1) of the Massachusetts residential code, 780 CMR, marks ice barrier underlayment as required statewide.

## What ice dams do to trim and siding

Most homeowners think of ice dams as a roof problem, but the water does not stay on the roof. Once it backs up under the roofing, it follows gravity. Places to check in late winter and spring:

- **Fascia and rake boards.** These sit right at the roof edge, where the dam forms and where gutters overflow with ice.
- **Soffits.** Water that gets past the roof edge can drip into the soffit area. Look for stains, sagging panels or peeling paint underneath.
- **Upper siding courses and corner boards.** Water running down behind siding can show up as streaks, swelling or paint failure high on the wall.
- **Window and door heads.** Water that gets inside the wall can show up around openings. Stains on interior window trim or casing are worth a closer look.
- **Gutters.** Heavy ice can pull gutters away from the fascia, which opens another path for water.

Wood that stays wet season after season can soften and rot. Paint that keeps blistering in the same spot under an eave is often a moisture sign worth investigating.

## Short-term steps during the winter

The National Weather Service suggests:

- Keeping snow on the roof to a minimum, using a roof rake from the ground.
- Keeping gutters and downspouts clear of snow and ice.
- Cleaning leaves and debris out of gutters in the fall so meltwater can drain.

Stay off icy roofs. If water is already coming inside, call a roofing professional.

## Fixing the cause

Removing snow helps in the moment. The lasting fix is reducing the heat that reaches the roof:

1. **Air sealing.** Seal the gaps where warm air leaks from the living space into the attic. University of Minnesota Extension puts this step first.
2. **Insulation.** The National Weather Service suggests attic insulation of at least R-30, with R-38 preferred in northern climates.
3. **Ventilation.** Attic venting keeps the roof deck cold, which reduces the freeze and thaw cycles that build dams.
4. **Ice barrier at the eaves.** The Massachusetts code table marks ice barrier as required. The model residential code language describes it running from the lowest roof edges to a point at least 24 inches inside the exterior wall line. When the roof is next replaced, ask your roofer how they will meet the requirement on your house.

Air sealing, insulation and roofing are separate trades from siding and trim. Plan the attic work first, so the trim and siding repair is not undone by the next winter.

## Repairing the damage

Once the cause is addressed, repair the exterior so it sheds water again:

- Replace rotted fascia, rake, soffit and corner boards.
- Pull back siding where staining suggests water got behind it, and check the sheathing and weather barrier.
- Re-flash window and door heads that were taking on water.
- Reattach or replace gutters that ice pulled loose.

Doing the repair before the cause is fixed often means paying twice. Doing the cause and leaving rotted trim in place lets water keep getting in at the weak spots.
