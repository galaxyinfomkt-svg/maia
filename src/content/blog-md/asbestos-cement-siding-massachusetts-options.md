Many older houses in Massachusetts still wear the hard, brittle siding shingles that were put on decades ago, often painted over more than once. Some of those shingles are asbestos cement. If you are planning new siding, that possibility changes the order of the job: you need to know what the material is, and what the state requires, before anyone pries off the first course.

This article covers what to do first, the three paths homeowners usually weigh, and the questions to settle with the state before you choose one.

## Step one: do not guess, and do not break it

EPA says you generally cannot tell whether a material contains asbestos just by looking at it, unless it is labeled. Its homeowner guidance is simple:

- If you suspect a material contains asbestos, do not touch it.
- Taking samples yourself is not recommended.
- Material that is in good condition and will not be disturbed should be left alone.
- Workers you hire to handle asbestos should be trained and accredited.

A trained inspector can take samples and arrange laboratory analysis. Until you have a result, treat the siding as if it contains asbestos.

## What Massachusetts requires

Two state agencies are involved.

### MassDEP: notice and work practices

The Massachusetts Department of Environmental Protection regulates asbestos under 310 CMR 7.15. Where the rule applies, a notification form (AQ 04, also called ANF-001) must be filed with MassDEP at least **ten working days** before removing or disturbing roofing or siding that contains asbestos or is presumed to.

The regulation also sets work practices for this material. It says asbestos cement shingles, siding and panels **shall not be broken, sanded, sawed or drilled** at any time during removal or later handling. In practice, that means the shingles are supposed to come off whole.

The rule includes some exemptions, including limited ones for owner-occupied single-family homes and for small amounts of exterior asbestos cement siding. Those exemptions have conditions, they have changed over the years, and the regulation says all its other requirements still apply. Before relying on any exemption, read the current 310 CMR 7.15 text or call your MassDEP regional office.

### DLS: who does the work

The Department of Labor Standards regulates asbestos work and worker training under 454 CMR 28.00, which replaced the older 453 CMR 6.00. A MassDEP FAQ says that for a definitive answer on whether a DLS-licensed asbestos contractor is required to remove asbestos roofing and siding, you should contact DLS. DLS lists its licensing office at 617-626-6960.

> Settle three things before choosing a path: what the lab says the material is, whether MassDEP notice is required for your job, and whether DLS requires a licensed asbestos contractor.

## The three paths homeowners weigh

### 1. Leave it alone

If the siding is in good condition and you are not planning any work that disturbs it, EPA's guidance supports leaving it in place. Keep it painted, avoid drilling or power-washing it, and plan for the day when you do want to change the exterior.

This path does not work well if shingles are cracked, missing or loose, or if you are about to replace windows, doors or trim that are surrounded by the siding.

### 2. Remove it, then install new siding

Removal clears the wall so the sheathing, flashing and weather barrier can be inspected and repaired before new siding goes on. It also means the next owner does not inherit the question.

Removal is regulated work. Expect testing, any required MassDEP notice, whatever DLS requires for the crew, intact removal under the work practice rules, and proper disposal. Those steps add time before the new siding can start, so build them into your schedule.

### 3. Cover it with new siding

Some homeowners ask about installing new siding over the old shingles. Before choosing that path, ask MassDEP and DLS how they view it for your house. New siding is fastened through to the wall, and the state rule bars drilling asbestos cement siding during removal and handling. You need a clear answer on whether your overlay method counts as disturbance, and on what work practices would apply, before you sign a contract.

Covering also leaves the asbestos in the wall. A future remodel, a window change or a later siding job will face the same question again.

## How to decide

Use these questions with whoever you hire:

1. Has the siding been sampled and tested by a qualified inspector?
2. If it contains asbestos, is MassDEP notice required for this job, and who files it?
3. Does DLS require a licensed asbestos contractor for this work?
4. Who handles removal and disposal, and how does that fit the siding schedule?
5. If covering is proposed, what did MassDEP or DLS say about it?
6. Was the house built before 1978? If so, painted surfaces may also fall under [lead-safe renovation rules](/blog/lead-safe-siding-window-work-pre-1978-massachusetts).

Get the answers in writing and keep copies of any notification and disposal paperwork with your house records.
