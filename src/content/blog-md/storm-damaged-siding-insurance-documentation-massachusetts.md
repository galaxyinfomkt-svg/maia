After a windstorm you walk around the house and find a row of vinyl panels hanging loose, a corner post cracked by a falling limb, or siding torn off near the roofline. Your first thought is to get it fixed. Your second is whether insurance will pay, and how to avoid doing something that makes the claim harder.

The Massachusetts Division of Insurance publishes guidance for homeowners after storm damage. This article puts that guidance in order for a siding claim and adds the practical steps that help an adjuster see what happened.

## What standard policies usually cover

The Division's disaster FAQ says damage caused by wind, wind-driven rain, damage from trees or other falling objects, and collapse due to the weight of ice or snow are all covered under most standard homeowners policies. Your own policy, deductible and exclusions decide your case, so the Division's first piece of advice is to know your policy: what is covered, what is excluded, and what your deductible is.

## In the first hours: safety, then the insurer

1. **Stay safe.** The Division says not to re-enter your home or make repairs until it is safe. Stay away from downed power lines and report them to the power company.
2. **Call your insurer or agent as soon as possible.** The Division notes that your policy might require you to report within a certain time frame.
3. **Photograph everything before you touch it.** The Division advises taking photographs or video of the damage before making temporary repairs, so the record shows how things looked.

## How to document siding damage

Good photos and notes make it easier for an adjuster to connect the damage to the storm. A useful set includes:

- **Wide shots of each side of the house**, so the damaged area can be located on the building.
- **Medium shots** of the damaged section, showing how far it extends.
- **Close-ups** of cracks, holes, missing panels, bent corner posts and torn trim, with something for scale if you can.
- **The cause, if visible:** the fallen limb, a neighbor's debris, or panels found in the yard.
- **Inside the house**, any water stains on walls or ceilings behind the damaged siding.
- **Date and time.** Most phone cameras record this automatically; keep the original files.

Keep any pieces of siding that blew off. The Division's guidance says to keep damaged property for the adjuster to inspect.

### Record the storm itself

Write down the date and approximate time of the storm. NOAA's Storm Events Database publishes National Weather Service Storm Data, which NCEI describes as a collection of significant weather events with enough intensity to cause property damage. It can be searched by map or by state and area. The database is not instant; when we checked in November 2026 it held data through July 2026. For a recent storm, local news reports and your own photos with time stamps are your first record, and the database can confirm the event later.

## Temporary repairs: yes. Permanent repairs: wait

The Division's guidance draws a clear line here.

- **Temporary repairs are expected.** The Division says policies might require you to make temporary repairs to protect your property from further damage, and that most policies cover the reasonable cost of emergency clean-up and temporary repairs. The Division gives boarding up damaged windows and patching holes in walls or roofs as examples of repairs that are appropriate even before you have seen the company representative.
- **Keep every receipt** for labor and materials of temporary repairs, and tell the adjuster what you did.
- **Do not make permanent repairs before the company inspects.** The Division states that an insurance company may deny a claim if you make permanent repairs before inspection.

Exposed sheathing should be covered quickly, because wet sheathing and insulation can lead to mold. The EPA advises drying water-damaged areas within 24 to 48 hours to prevent mold growth.

## Before and during the adjuster's visit

The Division suggests a few steps that help:

- **Get a repair estimate before you meet the adjuster**, if possible, so you know what the repair is likely to involve.
- **Provide records of improvements** you have made to the property, such as when the siding was installed.
- **Keep copies of all correspondence** and write down the date, name and title of everyone you speak with and what was said.
- **Ask for an itemized explanation** of any settlement offer.
- **If the contractor finds hidden damage** or the repair cost is in dispute, the Division says the fastest way through the process is to have your contractor and the adjuster meet at the home at the same time.

Massachusetts consumers can also hire a public insurance adjuster, according to the Division.

## When the siding is discontinued

Storm damage may affect only part of a wall, and older vinyl profiles or colors may no longer be made. If the replacement will not match, raise that with the adjuster early. Ask the insurer for the specific policy language that applies, as the Division recommends for any disagreement, and get any denial in writing with the reason and policy language.

## If you disagree with the result

The Division's advice is to ask for the specific policy language in question, get a written denial letter if the claim is denied, and not rush into a settlement. If you still cannot resolve the dispute with the company, you can file a complaint with the Division of Insurance's Consumer Services Unit.

## Older houses

If your house was built before 1978, repairing siding and trim can disturb painted surfaces that may contain lead. In Massachusetts, paid exterior work that disturbs more than 20 square feet of painted surface is regulated under 454 CMR 22.00. Ask your contractor how the repair will be handled.
