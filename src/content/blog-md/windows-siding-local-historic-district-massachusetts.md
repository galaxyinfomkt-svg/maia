You found the windows you want and a contractor you trust, and then a neighbor mentions that your street is in a historic district. Now you are not sure whether you can change anything on the outside of your house without permission, how long that takes, or what happens if the work starts first.

In Massachusetts, local historic districts are set up under Chapter 40C of the General Laws. This article explains what that law requires before exterior work, what can be exempt, and how to prepare an application so the project stays on schedule.

## First, which kind of "historic" is your house?

Two very different designations get called "historic district," and they have different effects.

- **National Register district.** The Massachusetts Historical Commission (MHC) says National Register status in itself places no constraints on what owners may do with their property when using private funding, unless state or federal funds, permits or licenses are involved, or a local bylaw, ordinance or policy applies.
- **Local historic district (LHD).** Created by a city or town under Chapter 40C. MHC explains that in a local historic district, proposed changes to exterior architectural features visible from a public way are reviewed by a locally appointed historic district commission.

A property can be in both. If you only know that your street is "historic," ask your town's historic district commission or planning office which designation applies to your parcel.

### How common are local districts here?

The MHC inventory in MACRIS, published through MassGIS, lists designated areas town by town. When we queried it, Worcester had six inventory areas carrying a local historic district designation, including the Elm Street, Montvale, Massachusetts Avenue and Crown Hill historic districts. Charlton had three, all parts of the Northside Historic District. The inventory is a research tool. Your town's bylaw or ordinance and its district map are what control.

## What Chapter 40C requires

Section 6 of Chapter 40C says that, except as the local ordinance or bylaw provides under sections 8 and 9, no building or structure within a historic district shall be constructed or altered in any way that affects exterior architectural features unless the commission has first issued one of three certificates:

- **Certificate of appropriateness:** the commission finds the proposed change appropriate for the district.
- **Certificate of non-applicability:** the commission finds the work does not need its approval.
- **Certificate of hardship:** a certificate the commission can issue in hardship situations, under conditions the statute sets out.

Replacement windows, new siding, new doors and trim changes all alter exterior features, so in a local district you should assume the commission will want to see them unless your town's rules say otherwise.

### Work that may be exempt

Two sections of the law can narrow what needs review.

- **Section 9** says the chapter does not prevent ordinary maintenance, repair or replacement of an exterior architectural feature that does not involve a change in design, material, color or outward appearance. Replacing a rotted clapboard with a matching one would generally fit that description. Swapping wood windows for a different material or style generally would not, because it changes design or material. The commission has the final say on how it reads its own bylaw.
- **Section 8** lets a city or town limit the commission's review in its ordinance or bylaw. The categories listed in the law include storm windows and doors, screens, window air conditioners, lighting fixtures, antennae, the color of paint and the color of roofing materials.

Whether your town uses any of the section 8 limits depends on its own bylaw. Some towns publish design guidelines that spell this out. Plymouth's storm window and door guidelines, for example, call for the commission to approve aluminum storm window colors on non-historic buildings.

### Timing

Section 11 sets the review process. Under it, the commission must decide within 60 days after an application is filed, unless the applicant agrees in writing to a longer period, and a public hearing requires advance public notice. Town procedures vary within those rules. Acton and Foxborough, for example, describe scheduling a hearing within 45 days of filing and deciding within 60.

Town guidance, such as Foxborough's, also says that when a building permit is needed, the building commissioner directs the applicant to the historic district commission for a certificate first. In practice, plan for the certificate to come before the permit.

## How to prepare an application for windows or siding

The commission decides based on what you submit, so give it a complete picture of the work.

1. **Read your town's bylaw and design guidelines.** Look for what is exempt, what materials are acceptable, and whether there is an administrative review for minor work.
2. **Talk to commission staff before you file.** Ask whether they offer an informal review or a pre-application conversation.
3. **Photograph the existing conditions** on every elevation you plan to change, including close-ups of windows, trim and siding.
4. **Bring product information.** For windows, include the manufacturer's cut sheets showing frame profiles, grille pattern and how the new unit sits in the opening. For siding, include the profile, exposure and material.
5. **Explain repair versus replacement.** If some windows or clapboards can be repaired and others cannot, say so and show why.
6. **Mark up drawings or photos** so the commission can see exactly what will change.
7. **Build the review into your schedule.** Order custom windows after the certificate, not before.

## If your house is not in a district

If the town tells you that you are outside any local district, ask for that in writing or ask whether it issues a certificate of non-applicability. Note it in your project file in case the question comes up at sale.

## Older houses and lead paint

If your house was built before 1978, keep the lead rules in mind too. Massachusetts treats window replacement in pre-1978 housing as regulated lead-safe renovation work under 454 CMR 22.00, and exterior work that disturbs more than 20 square feet of painted surface is covered too. Ask your contractor how that will be handled alongside the commission's requirements.
