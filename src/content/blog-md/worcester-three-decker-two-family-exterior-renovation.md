If you own a two-family or a three-decker in Worcester, the exterior work can come in big pieces: up to three stories of siding, many windows, porches stacked on porches, and trim that has been painted many times. Add tenants, and maybe a child under six in one of the units, and you have questions most single-family owners never face: who has to be notified, which rules apply to the work, and how the job fits with the Massachusetts Lead Law.

This article lays out those rules in plain terms so you can plan the project and ask contractors the right questions.

## How common these buildings are in Worcester

The U.S. Census Bureau's American Community Survey (2020 to 2024 five-year estimates) counts 85,876 housing units in the city of Worcester. Of those, 7,433 are in two-unit buildings and 21,346 are in buildings with three or four units, which together is about one in three housing units in the city. The same survey estimates 35,040 units in buildings built in 1939 or earlier, and about two in three units in buildings built before 1970.

In other words, most of the city's housing was built well before 1978, the cutoff year for the state's lead rules.

## Two different sets of lead rules

People often mix these up. They are separate, and both can apply to the same building.

### 1. The Massachusetts Lead Law (for owners)

The state's Childhood Lead Poisoning Prevention Program explains that the Lead Law requires the removal or covering of lead paint hazards in homes built before 1978 where any children under 6 live. Lead paint hazards include loose lead paint, lead paint on windows and friction surfaces, and other surfaces accessible to children.

Key points from the state's guidance for owners:

- **Owners are responsible** for complying, including owners of rental property.
- **Liability:** if a child is poisoned by lead hazards where the child lives, the owner is legally responsible. An owner cannot avoid liability by having tenants sign an agreement accepting the presence of lead paint.
- **No discrimination:** you cannot evict, or refuse to rent to, anyone because of lead paint.
- **How to comply:** first hire a licensed lead inspector to test the home and record hazards. Deleading work must be done by a trained and licensed person. After a passing reinspection, you receive a letter of full deleading compliance.
- **Interim control:** an owner may have only urgent hazards corrected first, with a licensed risk assessor. After approval you receive a Letter of Interim Control and then have up to 2 years to reach full compliance.

The state's guidance also lists a Tenant Lead Law Notification form for rental properties, and financial help is available through tax credits, grants and loans. Contact the Childhood Lead Poisoning Prevention Program at (800) 532-9571 with questions.

### 2. Lead-safe renovation rules (for contractors)

When a contractor is paid to do work that disturbs paint in pre-1978 housing, the Massachusetts Department of Labor Standards (DLS) rules apply. DLS states that when renovating, repairing or painting a home built before 1978, a licensed lead-safe renovation contractor must be used. The regulation is 454 CMR 22.00.

The regulation's definition of minor repair and maintenance helps show what is covered. Small jobs that disturb a total of 20 square feet or less of painted exterior surface can fall under that minor category, but **window replacement is excluded from it**. On a three-decker, siding, porch and trim work will usually pass 20 square feet quickly, and any window replacement counts regardless of size.

## Renovation is not deleading

New windows or new siding do not by themselves give you a Lead Law compliance letter. Only a licensed lead inspector or risk assessor can confirm compliance, and deleading work has its own licensing.

If you have, or expect, a child under six in a unit, the order of operations matters. Talk to a licensed lead inspector or risk assessor before you finalize the exterior scope. Ask whether replacing windows or covering certain surfaces could be planned to fit the deleading or interim control plan, and who must do which parts of the work.

## Planning an exterior job with tenants in place

Beyond the lead rules, a multi-family exterior job needs some practical planning:

1. **Tell tenants early** about the schedule, which sides of the building will be worked on, and when windows in their unit will be out.
2. **Porches and egress.** If tenants use rear porches and stairs to get in and out, plan the work so they keep a safe route.
3. **Containment and cleanup.** Ask the contractor how they will contain paint debris on the ground and keep it out of yards, porches and open windows.
4. **Permits.** Check with the City of Worcester's building department about permits for the work.
5. **Historic districts.** Worcester has local historic districts. If your building is in one, exterior changes may need a certificate from the historic district commission before work begins.
6. **Phasing.** On a big building, doing one elevation at a time can spread the cost, but each phase still has to follow the lead-safe rules.

## Questions to ask contractors

- Do you hold a Massachusetts Lead-Safe Renovation Contractor license? What is the license number?
- How will you contain and clean up on a building with occupied units?
- How will you sequence windows, siding and trim so flashing laps correctly?
- What is your plan for porches and egress during the work?
- Will the written estimate break the job into phases if I ask?
