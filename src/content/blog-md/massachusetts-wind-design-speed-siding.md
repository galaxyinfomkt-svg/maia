After a strong nor'easter or a summer thunderstorm, it is common to see a few vinyl panels flapping on a house down the street, or a strip of soffit hanging loose. When that happens, homeowners often ask whether the siding was the wrong product. Sometimes it was. The other half of the answer is how it was fastened.

Massachusetts gives every town a design wind speed in its building code. Knowing your town's number, and what it means for siding, helps you ask better questions when you compare quotes.

## Where the number comes from

The Massachusetts residential code, 780 CMR (10th edition), includes **Table R301.2(2)**, which lists design values for every city and town in the state, including ground snow load and a design wind speed, written in the table as Vult.

A few examples from the table:

- **Charlton:** 118 mph
- **Worcester:** 117 mph
- **Framingham:** 119 mph
- **Holden and Princeton:** 116 mph
- **Woburn:** 118 mph

Across the state, the listed values run from 116 to 130 mph.

### What the number is, and what it is not

The design wind speed is an engineering input used to calculate the pressures a building and its cladding have to resist. It is not a forecast, and it does not mean winds of that speed are expected at your house in a typical year. Designers use it, along with other factors in the code, to set the wind pressure that wall coverings and their fasteners must handle.

> Your town's design wind speed is a design value from the state code. Your siding product and its installation instructions translate it into how the panels are fastened.

## How the code connects wind to siding

The residential code does not leave siding to guesswork.

### Products must be certified

A code reference chart published by the vinyl siding industry's trade association summarizes the International Residential Code requirements this way: vinyl siding must be certified and labeled to show it conforms to its ASTM standard, **ASTM D3679**. Insulated vinyl siding has its own standard, ASTM D7793, and polypropylene siding uses ASTM D7254.

The association runs a product certification program, accredited under ISO/IEC 17065, and lists certified products in Intertek's public directory.

### Installation follows a table and the manufacturer

The same chart notes that IRC Table R703.3(1) sets out prescriptive and performance installation requirements for wall coverings, and that vinyl siding is generally installed with roofing nails at 16 inches on center, with variations allowed. Manufacturers' instructions control the details.

Product evaluation reports carry this further. A published ICC-ES evaluation report for one manufacturer's vinyl siding and soffit, for example, says the design wind pressure must be determined under the code (IRC Section R301.2.1 for houses) and must not exceed the allowable values in the report's table. The same report specifies the nail head and shank size for attaching the panels.

### Soffit gets its own rules

Soffit hangs under the roof overhang, open to wind from below, and the code treats it separately. According to the trade association's chart, the IRC requires vinyl soffit panels to be designed for the appropriate pressure in high-wind areas, to be fastened at both the fascia and the wall, and to have no unsupported span longer than 16 inches without an intermediate nailing strip.

## What this means for your project

A siding job that holds up in a storm comes down to three things that are easy to check:

1. **A certified product.** Ask for the product name and confirm it carries the certification label for its ASTM standard.
2. **A wind rating that fits.** Ask the contractor to show you the product's published wind or design pressure rating and how it compares with what the code requires for your house.
3. **Fastening per the instructions.** Nail type, nail spacing and how far nails penetrate into the wall all come from the manufacturer's installation instructions and evaluation report. Ask which document the crew will follow.

### Fiber cement, cedar and other materials

Fiber cement and wood siding follow the same idea. Manufacturers publish installation instructions that cover fastener types and spacing. Ask whether the product also has an evaluation report, and if you are choosing between materials, ask to see the fastening requirements for each one at your town's design wind speed.

### Repairs after a storm

If panels came loose, look past the damaged piece. Ask what caused the failure: missing fasteners, the wrong nails, an unsupported soffit span or a product that was never rated for the wall. Replacing a panel with the same problem in place sets up the same failure.

## Questions to ask when comparing quotes

- What design wind speed does the code list for my town?
- What product is being installed, and is it certified to its ASTM standard?
- What is the product's published wind or design pressure rating?
- Which installation manual or evaluation report will the crew follow?
- How will soffit be supported and fastened?

Clear answers to these questions, in writing, make quotes much easier to compare.
